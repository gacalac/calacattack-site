What is an interaction diagram?

Interaction Diagrams are a tool for identifying interactions between objects and defining “systems.”

We use Interaction Diagrams to identify a collection of objects that we will call the “system” and distinguish them from objects “outside the system” (i.e. in the surroundings).

The choice of a system boundary does not change anything about the event or interaction. It is just a tool we use to help analyze situations involving forces and energy transfers.

The way we choose a system boundary can help make our analysis easier but will vary depending on the questions we are trying to answer.

Checkpoint 1

Ana draws her system boundary around just the book. Ben draws his around the book and the brick sitting on it. Whose choice changes the forces acting on the book?

All objects are actually made up of smaller parts, e.g. molecules, atoms, electrons, protons and neutrons. So an “object” is really a system made up of smaller parts that we group together and analyze as a single “object”.

Other examples are cars, planes, people, etc. These “objects” are really systems of objects that we often analyze as if they were a single object.

Checkpoint 2

Your phone is really a system of thousands of parts. When does it make sense to treat it as a single object?

Steps in constructing an interaction diagram

  1. Identify the objects in the event that are interacting with each other. You know when two objects are interacting by determining if they influence each other.
  2. Most interactions occur when there is obvious contact between them. However, there are interactions that “act at a distance.” Magnets and the Earth can interact with objects they are not touching. These interactions involve forces that “act at a distance” through a magnetic or gravitational force field, which we will study in depth in later lessons.
Checkpoint 3

Which pair interacts without touching?

  1. Start making your Interaction Diagrams by drawing circles to represent each of the objects that are interacting. All objects are represented without any details of their shape or structure. Label each circle to identify the object that it represents.
Note

The placement and orientation of objects does not really matter, as you are just trying to identify interactions between objects. We will use another tool, called the Force Diagram, to show how interactions affect the system.

  1. Draw lines between objects that interact with each other with arrow heads on each end. Whenever an interaction line ends on a circle, this means that the object represented by that circle experiences a Force, push or pull, from the object at the other end of the line.
  1. Every force is an interaction between two objects. Both objects experience the same force, but in opposite directions, as indicated by the arrowheads. If object J is pushed by object K, then object K is also pushed by object J.
This is Newton's 3rd law

When two objects interact, each one pushes or pulls on the other with a force of equal strength in the opposite direction. Forces always come in these pairs, so every interaction line you draw shows one 3rd-law pair.

Example 1

If we are interested in analyzing the forces on the book only, we might define the system as just the book. System = Book. In this case the 3 interactions with the book all cross the system boundary. You should notice that the Brick does NOT interact with the floor directly and thus there is no interaction line between them.

Example 2

If we decided to analyze the Brick and Book as a system, we would include both within the system boundary. The Interaction Diagram would change as shown. This does not change the situation or event, just the way you are going to analyze it. The interaction between the Brick and Book is “internal” to the system and will be ignored in our force analysis, as you will see in future lessons.

System = Brick and Book. In this case some of the interactions cross the system boundaries (brick-earth, book-floor, book-earth). The only interaction that is inside the system is the one between the book and the brick.

Example 3

System = Brick, Book and Floor. In this case the only interactions that cross the system boundary are between the earth and the objects within the system. All other interactions are “internal” and will be ignored in our future force analysis.
