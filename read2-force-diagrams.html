Force diagrams and interaction diagrams

A proper force diagram (also called a free body diagram, or FBD) will include the following elements:

  • An interaction diagram that shows the object(s) that make up the system being analyzed (see Read 1).
  • A dotted line around the object(s), which denotes the elements of the system.
  • Each connection on the interaction diagram becomes an arrow on the force diagram (sometimes 2).
  • An arrow of appropriate length, in the proper direction to represent each force.
  • An appropriate label for each force.
  • All force arrows should start at the center of the object.

Balanced and unbalanced forces

Force diagrams can depict situations where the forces are balanced and situations where the forces are unbalanced. In this section we will explore the relationship between motion and these force situations that are responsible for them.

In other words, by identifying the type of motion we are dealing with, we can determine whether the forces are balanced or unbalanced. Or, in reverse, if we know the forces are balanced or unbalanced, we can determine the type of motion.

There are five major motion situations that we will deal with in this unit. These include:

  1. Objects at rest (balanced forces).
  2. Objects moving at a constant speed in a straight line (balanced forces).
  3. Objects that are speeding up (unbalanced forces).
  4. Objects that are slowing down (unbalanced forces).
  5. Objects that are changing direction (unbalanced forces).

Situations 1 & 2: at rest and uniform motion

Objects at rest and objects undergoing uniform motion are both examples of objects that experience no unbalanced force. In other words, the forces acting on an object must be balanced for an object to be at rest or for one to move at a constant speed in a straight line (aka, constant velocity).

Balanced force diagrams

A bowling ball sits perfectly still on a perfectly level gym floor. The earth pulls downward on it with a gravitational force. We denote this force as FGravity on Ball by Earth. The ball doesn't fall as a result of this force, so there must be a force balancing the gravitational force. That force is from the floor pushing upward on the ball. We denote the upward push on the ball by the floor FNormal on Ball by Floor.

The pictorial diagram for this situation must show the ball and the floor. Superimposed on the pictorial diagram is the force diagram. The two forces acting on the object are the same magnitude, but act in opposite directions, so they are balanced. Mathematically, this means that their sum is zero. Therefore, the sum of the forces (aka net force) acting on the ball is zero.

The forces are balanced, therefore the object's motion is NOT changing (sitting still, acceleration = 0).

When we label forces, we want to indicate the type of interaction between the objects, what object the force is acting on and what object the force is caused by. Therefore, we will use the following notation:

Force labels
Ftype on feeler by dealer

Sometimes written Fkind on victim by agent. Example: FNormal on Ball by Floor

Normal type of force. Here: a surface pushing. Others: Gravity, Applied (a push or pull by a person)
on Ball the feeler: the ball feels this force
by Floor the dealer: the floor exerts it
Checkpoint 3

A book rests on a table. Earth pulls down on the book (the red arrow). Which label follows “type on feeler by dealer”?

Let us now assume that the bowling ball is already rolling to the right. (How it started moving is outside our observation window, so we cannot determine it.) What forces act on the ball right now? Interestingly, the force diagram is identical to the previous situation. The ball experiences only the downward force of gravity due to the earth FGravity on Ball by Earth, and the upward force on the ball by the floor FNormal on Ball by Floor. The forces remain balanced. And so, mathematically the net force on the ball is still zero (balanced force).

The most important thing to understand about this situation is that the ball will, as long as the forces remain balanced, travel at the same speed in a straight line. Nothing is required to cause the ball to keep moving. An unbalanced force would be required to cause it to go faster or slower, and/or an unbalanced force would be necessary to change its direction. But in the absence of an unbalanced force, the ball will continue to move in a straight line at a constant speed. In reality there is a little bit of friction, so the ball will slow eventually. But since friction is so small, we can often consider it to be negligible and leave it off the force diagram.

This fact of nature, that objects will maintain their state of motion or rest, as long as there is no unbalanced force on them, is sometimes called Newton's 1st Law of Motion. Objects have mass, which is a measure of the object's resistance to changing its motion, often called inertia. The more massive something is, the harder it is to change its motion. Therefore, an object's inertia has to do with how much mass it has and is sometimes used as the definition for mass. It has nothing to do with a force. Inertia is not a cause of anything, and we don't want you to think it is.

A bowling ball rolls to the right at constant speed across the gym floor (ignore friction). What keeps it moving?

Unbalanced force: speeding up

Consider a bowling ball, which is at rest on a level gym floor but has a person pushing it to the right. The downward force of gravity on the ball, FGravity on Ball by Earth, and the upward push of the floor on the ball, FNormal on Ball by Floor, still act on the ball. Additionally, the person is exerting a force (by pushing with their hand) on the ball to the right. Let's call that force FApplied on Ball by Hand. The first two forces act in opposite directions and are balanced, but the new force, FApplied on Ball by Hand, is an unbalanced force.

The result of this unbalanced force, or any unbalanced force for that matter, is that the object in question will accelerate. This is to say that the object will speed up, slow down, or change direction. In this case, the unbalanced force will cause the object to begin to move in the direction of the force. After it starts moving, the unbalanced force acts in the same direction as the object is moving and the result is that the object will speed up. Anytime the unbalanced force on an object acts in the same direction the object is traveling, the object will speed up in the direction of the net force.

The arrows above the images of the ball are intended to convey the changing speed of the ball. In this case the length of the arrows represents the speed of the ball and the direction the arrows point represents the direction the ball is moving.

Whenever an object experiences an unbalanced force in the same direction it is moving, it will speed up!

Unbalanced force: slowing down

Now let's consider a different situation. In this case, the bowling ball is already rolling to the right. A person pushes the ball to the left, while it is rolling toward her. Remember, the ball is moving to the right while being pushed to the left. How will this affect the motion of the ball? Analysis of the force diagram indicates that the unbalanced force is FApplied on Ball by Hand. In this case, and any time the direction of the unbalanced force is opposite the direction of motion, the object will slow down.

Notice that the images of the ball are getting closer together, indicating that the ball is slowing down. The arrows, indicating velocity, are getting shorter as well.

Whenever an object experiences an unbalanced force opposite the direction it is moving, it will slow down!

Shrinking the object to a point

Typically in a Force Diagram, we do not draw the image of the object (or system of objects), but rather shrink it down to a point because the shape of the system does not matter when analyzing forces.

Acceleration toward right

Acceleration toward left

The force diagram shows which way the object accelerates, not which way it moves. “Acceleration toward right” could be speeding up while moving right, or slowing down while moving left.
